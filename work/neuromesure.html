Neuromesure turns free-text reports of what a person was thinking into structured time-use data. A language model labels each thought with a taxonomy drawn from published cognitive-science frameworks, and deterministic functions compute descriptive, time-weighted metrics. It measures and shows data; it does not give advice.

What is measured

  • Eight modes, which can overlap: focused, deciding, exploring, reflecting, anticipating, looping, relating and maintaining.
  • Dynamics: directed, free or stuck, following the dynamic framework of spontaneous thought (Christoff et al., 2016).
  • Time orientation (past, present, future or mixed), origin and topic.
  • Duration, from start and end times or from phrases such as "two hours", read by a rule-based parser without AI.
  • Self-reported results: whether the thinking led to a result, answered yes, no or unsure.

Method

A language model receives a fixed prompt with worked examples and returns structured labels, which the server checks against the allowed lists. All metrics are pure, unit-tested functions:

  • Time per mode, weighted by duration.
  • The grounded share: hours not spent in stuck or looping thought.
  • What stuck time was about, by topic.
  • A result rate per topic: hours answered "yes" divided by hours answered "yes" or "no".

Below five thoughts or two hours, the app shows "collecting" instead of a number. All comparisons are worded as "coincides", never "causes".

Grounding in the literature

The design draws on Christoff, Irving, Fox, Spreng and Andrews-Hanna (2016) for the dynamics of thought, Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010) on mind-wandering in daily life, and Watkins (2008) on constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought. The project notes where evidence is mixed, for example on mind-wandering and creativity.

Limitations

Neuromesure is a prototype measurement framework, not a validated psychometric instrument. Validation would need:

  • Accuracy against thoughts labelled by experts, with inter-rater agreement such as Cohen's kappa.
  • Reliability and factor structure for the calibration questionnaire.
  • Evidence that the labels relate to established measures, such as rumination scales.
  • Test sets kept separate from the prompt examples, a larger and more varied sample, and an outcome measure that is not self-reported.
